# Construction Trade Glossary

Plain-English definitions of common commercial construction and subcontracting terms, for
someone new to the industry. This is public industry vocabulary — no company, project, or
person is referenced anywhere in this file.

---

## Bidding and procurement

**ITB — Invitation to Bid**
A general contractor's request for subcontractors to submit a price on a defined scope of
work, usually with a hard due date and attached drawings and specifications.
*Why it matters:* ITBs are the main inbound flow of opportunities. Whoever is on the
distribution list receives them; whoever is not finds out late or not at all.

**Prequalification ("prequal")**
The vetting process a general contractor or public owner runs before allowing a
subcontractor to bid at all. Typically covers financial capacity, bonding, safety record,
insurance, relevant experience, and years in business.
*Why it matters:* it is a gate. An incomplete prequal package means no invitations,
regardless of how good the relationships are.

**Hard bid (competitive / lump sum)**
Award goes to the lowest responsive, responsible bidder on a fully designed project.
Little room for relationships; price decides.
*Why it matters:* margins are thin and win rates are low. Volume-driven.

**Negotiated work**
The owner or general contractor selects a contractor based on qualifications,
relationship, and past performance, then negotiates price — often before design is
complete.
*Why it matters:* this is where relationship-building actually pays. It is the reason the
long-horizon pipeline exists.

**CMAR — Construction Manager at Risk**
A delivery method where the construction manager joins early to advise during design, then
takes on the risk of delivering within a guaranteed maximum price.
*Why it matters:* subcontractors are often selected on a best-value basis rather than pure
low bid, and early involvement can matter more than price.

**Scope letter**
The document accompanying a bid that states precisely what is included and — just as
importantly — what is excluded, clarified, or assumed.
*Why it matters:* it is the primary defense against being held to work that was never
priced. Vague scope letters are how subcontractors absorb someone else's costs.

**Buyout**
The period after a general contractor wins a project when they finalize which
subcontractors actually get the work and at what number.
*Why it matters:* being low bidder does not mean having the job. Deals shift during
buyout, and being reachable and responsive during it matters.

**Addendum**
An official change or clarification issued during the bidding period, modifying the
drawings, specifications, or schedule.
*Why it matters:* missing one means bidding the wrong scope. They often arrive close to
the due date.

## Company qualifications

**EMR — Experience Modification Rate**
An insurance rating of a company's workers' compensation claims history relative to
industry average. 1.0 is average; below 1.0 is better than average.
*Why it matters:* many general contractors and owners set a maximum EMR for site access,
commonly around 1.0. It is a hard screen on prequalification forms and it is one of the
first numbers asked for.

**Bonding capacity**
The maximum amount a surety will bond, expressed both as a single-project limit and an
aggregate limit across all active work.
*Why it matters:* it sets the ceiling on project size. Pursuing work above capacity wastes
everyone's time.

**Backlog**
The value of work already under contract but not yet completed.
*Why it matters:* it signals both stability and available capacity. Too little suggests
trouble ahead; too much means no room to take on more.

**Retainage**
A percentage of each payment (often 5–10%) withheld until the work is complete and
accepted.
*Why it matters:* it is real money held for months and is a significant cash-flow factor
for subcontractors.

**HUB / DBE / MBE**
Certification programs for historically underutilized, disadvantaged, or minority-owned
business enterprises.
*Why it matters:* public projects frequently carry participation goals, so certification
can be a genuine competitive advantage on publicly funded work.

## People on a project

**Chief estimator**
Runs the general contractor's estimating department; owns the bid number and decides which
subcontractor pricing gets carried.
*Why it matters:* often the single most important relationship for a subcontractor
pursuing hard-bid work.

**Preconstruction manager ("precon")**
Handles the phase before construction starts — budgeting, constructability review,
planning, early subcontractor involvement.
*Why it matters:* precon is where negotiated work is shaped, long before an ITB exists.
Being known here is upstream of being invited later.

**Superintendent ("super")**
Runs the jobsite day to day: sequencing, coordination, and crews in the field.
*Why it matters:* they have significant informal influence over whether a subcontractor
gets asked back. Field performance reputation travels through supers.

**Project executive**
Senior manager accountable for a project or portfolio commercially and for the client
relationship.
*Why it matters:* relevant for larger relationship decisions rather than individual bids.

## Concrete and structural scopes

**Tilt-wall (tilt-up)**
Large concrete wall panels cast flat on site, then lifted ("tilted") into place with a
crane to form the building's walls.
*Why it matters:* it is the dominant construction method for warehouse, distribution, and
light industrial buildings. Whether a concrete contractor self-performs tilt-wall largely
determines whether that entire market segment is addressable.

**Post-tension (PT)**
A slab reinforced with steel tendons that are tensioned after the concrete cures,
allowing longer spans and thinner slabs.
*Why it matters:* a specialized capability with its own crews and equipment. It does not
come automatically with general concrete experience.

**Superflat / FF-FL floors**
Floors built to tight flatness (FF) and levelness (FL) tolerances, measured and certified
against a specified standard.
*Why it matters:* required where high-reach forklifts and automated equipment operate,
such as distribution centers. It demands specific equipment and technique, and the
tolerances are contractually enforced.

**Drilled piers (caissons)**
Deep foundation elements formed by drilling a shaft and filling it with reinforced
concrete, used to carry loads to competent soil or rock.
*Why it matters:* another distinct specialty. Sounds adjacent to general concrete work but
requires entirely different equipment and expertise.

**Flatwork**
Horizontal concrete: slabs on grade, sidewalks, driveways, and paving.

**Structural concrete**
Load-bearing concrete elements — columns, beams, elevated slabs, and walls that carry the
building.

**Sitework**
Site preparation before and around the building: grading, excavation, utilities, and
drainage.

## A note on scope adjacency

Several terms above — tilt-wall, post-tension, superflat, drilled piers — describe work
that *sounds* like a natural extension of general concrete capability but requires
different equipment, crews, and experience. A contractor competent in one may not perform
the others at all.

This is why capability claims are treated as a hard boundary in
[`system/GUARDRAILS.md`](system/GUARDRAILS.md): plausibility is not evidence, and
overstating a self-performed scope is the fastest permanent way to lose a general
contractor's trust.
