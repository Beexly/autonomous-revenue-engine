This one is different, so let me explain it before you open it.

An ordinary website is a stack of pages. You click, a new page loads, you click
again. The three above all work that way, and they work well.

The Table is one room instead. You open it and you are looking at a long table
set in candlelight, with your boards, your cart and your photographs laid out
on it. You never leave that room. Instead of loading a new page, the view moves
along the table to whatever the visitor wants to see next: the tables, the
cart, you, the prices. There is nothing for anyone to learn. They move, and it
follows. On a phone it settles into one still picture of the room, with the
same words.

I did not invent this. Before building it I studied 48 of the sites that win
the web's design awards — the studios behind them are names like Lusion and
North Kingdom — and The Table follows the same patterns. Almost nobody in
catering has one. It is the kind of thing a guest remembers and mentions.

than one. If you open it and it is not you, say so plainly. One of the three

THE PART THAT ACTUALLY GETS YOU BOOKED

A website brings the request in. What happens after the request arrives is
where the booking is won or lost, and that is the piece I want to make sure you
understand, because it is the one that pays for itself.

Here is how it works. A message is written ahead of time, in your words, and it
sits on your phone. When something happens — a quote comes in, an event ends, a
season is coming — your phone reminds you, you read the message, and you tap
send. Nothing ever goes out on its own. Nothing sounds like a machine. It is
your voice, every time. It just is not you at eleven at night trying to
remember who you owe a reply to.

There are six of them:

  1. Never miss a quote. A request from your site lands on your phone with the
     name, the date and the headcount already in it. If nobody has replied in a
     day, you get a nudge.

  2. The quiet quote. Three days on, if a quote has gone quiet, a short and
     friendly follow-up is ready. Silence is not a no.

  3. Confirmed and counted. Two days before the event: date, table, final
     headcount and arrival time, in writing. The headcount is the price, so
     this one protects your money.

  4. Turn every event into a review. A thank-you with a photo from their table
     two days after, and the Google review link the day after that. You have 24
     five-star reviews on The Knot and WeddingWire already. This builds the
     same proof on Google, where people actually search.

  5. Bring the host back. Thirty days later, one note to the same host, with
     whatever offer you decide. Someone who has already seen your table does
     not need convincing.

  6. The season ahead. Six weeks before each busy season — holidays,
     graduations, weddings — your past hosts hear from you first. Calendars
     fill in the order people are asked.

And that is only the messages. The $500 also covers:

  - Every one of the six written with you, sitting down together, in your voice
  - Set up on your phone, with the reminder on the day, so nothing slips
  - The log: every quote request that comes in, and whether it was answered
  - One screen a month: calls, quotes, bookings, and your best photo
  - Yours for good. No monthly fee, no software to learn, no chatbot.

Your smallest grazing table is $1,200. One booking that would otherwise have
slipped away pays for all six of these twice over.
